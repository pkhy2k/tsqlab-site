I built the website this column appears on. More precisely, I built it with AI.

I am not a developer. Until I made this website, I had never used a tool called Git and had barely heard of a service called Cloudflare. Yet the Lab's website now runs on its own domain, regularly publishes articles on tourism policy and hotel industry trends, and can be read in both Korean and English. AI helps with much of the work of drafting articles, drawing charts, translating into English and publishing them on the site.

Until very recently, this was unimaginable. Making a website meant finding a developer, getting a quote and waiting months. All of that has shrunk to a few conversations. It is remarkable and convenient. But to be honest, behind that convenience a question resurfaced, much like the one I felt long ago at the front desk: if AI can do all this, what is left for people?

## What has collapsed is the barrier of "can you do it?"

Building the website made one thing clear. What AI has broken down is the barrier of whether you know how to do something. You can now produce results without knowing how to code, polish English sentences or draw charts. The tourism industry is no different. AI is already part of answering guest inquiries, replying to reviews, writing reports and forecasting rates.

The pace of change shows in the numbers. In the World Economic Forum (WEF) *Future of Jobs Report 2025*, employers expected 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030, and named AI and big data as the fastest-growing skills. The hotel industry is already deep into this. In an overseas study the Lab reported on, 91% of hotel chains were using AI ([91% of Hotel Chains Use AI, but Only 13% Report Measurable Returns: Readiness, Not Adoption, Is the Problem](/posts/hotel-chain-ai-readiness-2026/)).

So in an age when "knowing how" is no longer an advantage, what should tourism workers hold on to?

## What has not collapsed: what, whether it is right, and who answers for it

Running the website with AI, I found three things AI did not do for me.

First, **deciding what to make**. AI did not decide which articles to publish, whether to write the Lab's introduction in a stiff written style or a warm polite tone, or whether to show or hide view counts. AI offered several options, but I had to judge which one suited the Lab. Without someone to set the direction, AI goes nowhere, however fast it is.

Second, **judging whether the result is right**. What AI produced contained parts that looked plausible but needed fixing. To spot them, I had to know the field. Whether an article's numbers match the source, whether an expression is actually used in the industry, whether an interpretation fits the feel of the hotel front line: in the end, someone who knows the front line had to look. This is the same thing I feel every day in QA work. AI does not know how many guests a deluxe room holds, or what our hotel's cancellation penalty is.

Third, **taking responsibility**. The moment an article goes up on the website, the name on it is not AI's but the Lab's. If something is wrong, it is a person who apologises and corrects it. The same is true in front of guests. When room information given by AI turns out to be wrong, guests look not for the system but for a person at the hotel.

## How tourism workers can survive

Based on this experience, I would like to suggest five things to colleagues in the tourism industry who share the same concern.

- **Do not abandon the language of the front line**: The most valuable asset in the AI era is the sense built on the front line. Questions such as "When is a two-night guest's last night?" or "Where do international guests check the cancellation policy?" can only be asked by people who have been there. Good questions produce good AI results.
- **Try it yourself**: Fearing AI and knowing its limits from using it are entirely different. Start by making one guest notice for tomorrow, or this week's report, with AI. Only those who have used it know what to hand over and what to keep.
- **Become the person who verifies**: People who use AI output as it is are easily replaced by AI. People who can look at a result and say "this is wrong" become more necessary. The habit of recalculating numbers, checking against the source and asking for the basis is an advantage.
- **Deepen hospitality**: The more routine work moves to machines, the more human the work people do must become. A welcome that remembers a guest's name, and the instinct to notice an unexpected inconvenience first, cannot be delegated. That Korea ranked 12th in the Travel & Tourism Development Index but only 65th for hospitality reputation also suggests that much remains for people rather than technology ([The Shadow Behind 12th Place: What a Hospitality Reputation Rank of 65th Asks of Korean Lodging](/posts/ttdi-hospitality-reputation-2026/)).
- **Cross boundaries**: There is a wide gap between the front line and technology. People who know hotels and understand systems, or know service and can read data, are still rare. I saw that gap when I moved from the front desk to QA. For tourism workers, AI is the lowest bridge across that boundary.

> What AI replaces is part of the work, not the person who knows why that work is needed.

## Turning fear into questions

When I worked at a hotel front desk, I feared that kiosks would take over my job. That fear led me into the world of hotel IT, and today I have written a book about working with AI agents, built a website with AI and run a research lab. Looking back, what protected me was not learning technology first, but reaching for new tools without letting go of the sense I had built on the front line.

The way for tourism workers to survive the AI era is not to become faster than AI. It is to remain the people who can ask, in front of an answer AI has given, "Is this really right for the guest?" Only those who have worked on the front line can ask that question, and as long as that question exists, tourism remains an industry of people.
